§welcome§

Before you begin

A journal for looking

Reading about a palm line is not the same as finding it on a living hand. This journal exists to close that gap, one sheet at a time.

Everything in these pages asks you to do the same three things in the same order. Look, carefully and in good light. Describe what you see in plain words, before you reach for any meaning. Then, and only then, offer an interpretation, phrased so that it can be wrong. The sheets are structured to make the first two steps unavoidable, because they are the steps beginners skip.

The journal has three parts. Part one is a set of guided observation sheets, one for each region of the hand, from the whole hand down to the minor lines. Work through them once on your own hands, slowly. Part two is the reading templates: a full reading template, with three copies, and a quick reading log with six. Part three is for reflection: what you are learning about your own looking, a twelve-week practice tracker, and an index of every reading you make.

How to use it

  • Print the sheets you need, or write on a tablet. The print edition of this journal uses light paper to save ink.
  • Do the observation sheets on your own hands first, then on a willing friend's.
  • Use pencil. You will want to revise.
  • Fill the reading templates only after the observation sheets for that hand are done.

What this journal is not

It is not a reference of meanings, and it will not tell you what a hand "says". Where a sheet offers traditional associations, they are prompts for your own careful sentence, not answers. Nothing here predicts the future or bears on health, money, law, or love.

The Quick Start Guide gives the orientation this journal assumes: the map of the hand and the first three lines. The Foundations Handbook gives the full course behind every sheet. Both are at palmistrypath.com.

§method§

The method

Look · Describe · Hold it lightly

Three moves, in order, on every sheet.

I · Look

Angled light. Both hands. The whole hand before any part. Find the feature on the actual hand before you write a word about it.

II · Describe

Plain language only: where it begins, where it ends, its course, its depth, its clarity. "A deep curve ending between the first two fingers." No meanings yet.

III · Hold it lightly

Now the traditional reading, in its own words: traditionally associated with, often interpreted as, may suggest. Then ask what the rest of the hand says.

Three habits worth keeping

  • Write the boring sentence first. Every interpretive leap in this journal is preceded by a description field. Fill it. The description is the reading; the interpretation is commentary on it.
  • Name what stands out. Each sheet asks for the single most striking feature before anything systematic. A reading that ignores the obvious has gone wrong before it started.
  • Let features disagree. A warm heart line on a reserved hand is not a contradiction to resolve. It is an observation to record. The full reading template gives you a place to hold both.

Few and deep? Many and fine? A palm crowded with small lines, or a quiet one?

Hold it lightly

A less clear line is read in every tradition as a matter of quality, not fate. Chaining, islands, and breaks describe how a line is drawn, and are traditionally interpreted as variation or unsettledness in what the line is associated with, never as an event or a warning.

Hold it lightly

A full mount is traditionally read as strength in whatever the mount is associated with, and a flat one as its quieter expression. Neither is better. The mounts are context for the lines that cross them, and are read together with them in the full reading template.

§s16§

Phrasing guide and quick glossary

Sheet 16
Reference

Keep this page beside you when you write the interpretive fields.

Traditionally associated with

Reports what an inherited tradition says. A fact about the tradition, not about the person.

Often interpreted as

Offers a common reading while leaving room for others. Most sentences belong here.

May suggest

A feature points somewhere but the rest of the hand has not confirmed it. The weakest claim, and often the truest.

Leave out entirely: will, always, never, means that you, and anything about health, money, legal matters, relationships as advice, or the length of a life.

Active hand
The hand you write with; traditionally, what practice has made of disposition.
Passive hand
The other hand; traditionally, disposition and inheritance.
Percussion
The outer edge of the palm, below the little finger.
Plain of Mars
The hollow at the centre of the palm.
Mount
A fleshy pad, named for a planet in the Western tradition; read for fullness and firmness.
Luna
The outer heel of the palm, toward which a sloping head line travels.
Quality
Depth, clarity, and continuity of a line: deep, faint, chained, broken, feathered.
Island
A small loop within a line where it splits and rejoins.
Fork
A line dividing into two at its end.
Branch
A smaller line leaving a main line and continuing on its own.
Sister line
A second line running parallel and close to a major line.
Rascettes
The creases across the wrist.
