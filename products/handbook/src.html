Before you begin

How to use this book

This is a course, not a dictionary. It is built to be read in order, with your own hands open beside it.

The handbook follows the order a reading follows. Part one teaches you how to look: what palmistry is and is not, the sequence a reading moves through, the shape and texture of the hand, the thumb and fingers, and the two hands compared. Part two takes the four major lines one at a time, each with its variations drawn and explained. Part three walks the landscape of the mounts. Part four covers the minor lines, the simian line, and then the two chapters that make the book a course rather than a catalogue: how to combine what you see, and how to give a reading to another person.

Each chapter ends the same way. A Try it panel gives you a hands-on exercise to do before moving on. A Key ideas box summarises what the chapter established. Where the traditions genuinely disagree, an Across traditions panel says so, because pretending to a consensus that does not exist would teach you less, not more.

Reading the book

  • Read part one before anything else, even if the lines are what drew you here.
  • Do every Try it exercise on your own hands. The Practice Journal gives you sheets for recording them.
  • Treat the variation plates as things to find, not things to memorise.
  • Read chapters 24 and 25 twice: once now, once after you have read a few real hands.

The sources

Where a reading is attributed to Cheiro, Benham, Gettings, West, or Fincham, that is because the reading comes from them and can be checked in their books. Where no name is given, the association is common to the Western tradition and no single author owns it. Nothing here has been invented to fill a gap. The full list is in the appendix.

The phrasing this book uses

You will meet three phrases on nearly every page. Traditionally associated with reports what an inherited tradition says a feature means; it is a fact about the tradition, not about the person. Often interpreted as offers a common reading while leaving room for others. May suggest is the weakest claim, used when a feature points somewhere the rest of the hand has not yet confirmed. Learn to use them yourself. A reading built from these phrases can be wrong without being harmful, and that is the standard this book holds itself to.

Appendix A

Glossary

The vocabulary of the book, in one place.

Active hand
The hand a person writes with. Traditionally read as showing what practice and circumstance have made of a disposition.
Apollo
The ring finger, and the mount beneath it.
Branch
A smaller line leaving a main line and continuing on its own course.
Chained
A line made of many small linked loops rather than one clean stroke.
Chirognomy
The reading of the hand's shape, texture, and structure, as distinct from its lines.
Chiromancy
The reading of the lines. Together with chirognomy, the two halves of palmistry.
Consistency
How the palm responds to pressure: soft, elastic, firm, or hard.
Fate line
The vertical line up the centre of the palm toward the middle finger. Present on some hands, faint or absent on many.
Fork
A line dividing into two at its end.
Girdle of Venus
A curved minor line above the heart line, beneath the middle and ring fingers.
Head line
The major line crossing the centre of the palm, beginning on the thumb side.
Heart line
The uppermost major line, running beneath the fingers from the percussion edge.
Island
A small loop within a line, where it splits and rejoins.
Jupiter
The index finger, and the mount beneath it.
Life line
The major line curving around the ball of the thumb. Not a measure of lifespan.
Luna
The mount at the outer heel of the palm, opposite Venus.
Marriage lines
Short horizontal lines on the percussion edge below the little finger. Also called relationship or affection lines.
Mars, Lower
The mount inside the life line's origin, between the thumb and index finger.
Mars, Upper
The mount on the percussion edge between the heart and head lines.
Mars, Plain of
The hollow at the centre of the palm.
Mercury
The little finger, and the mount beneath it. Also the name of a minor line rising toward it.
Mount
A fleshy pad on the palm, named for a planet in the Western tradition, read for fullness and firmness.
Passive hand
The hand a person does not write with. Traditionally read as disposition and inheritance.
Percussion
The outer edge of the palm, below the little finger.
Phalange
One of the sections of a finger or thumb between its joints.
Quality
The depth, clarity, and continuity of a line.
Rascettes
The creases across the wrist at the base of the palm.
Ring of Solomon
A small curved minor line around the base of the index finger.
Saturn
The middle finger, and the mount beneath it.
Simian line
A single line crossing the palm where the heart and head lines would ordinarily run separately.
Sister line
A second line running parallel and close to a major line.
Sun line
A minor line rising toward the ring finger. Also called the Apollo line.
Venus
The mount at the ball of the thumb, enclosed by the life line.
Via lascivia
A minor line low on the palm, crossing toward the Mount of Luna.

Appendix B

Sources and further reading

The five Western references this book draws on by name, and a note on the traditions beyond them.

Cheiro (William John Warner), Palmistry for All, 1916
The most widely read popular palmistry book of its era, and the source of many readings that later authors either adopted or argued with. Confident to a fault; read alongside the others.
William G. Benham, The Laws of Scientific Hand Reading, 1900
A systematic, landmark-based method that insists on reading the whole hand before any line. Much of this book's emphasis on texture, consistency, and proportion follows Benham's ordering.
Fred Gettings, The Book of the Hand, 1965
A historically literate survey that is careful to distinguish traditions and to say where older claims are not supported. Valuable for its restraint.
Peter West, The Complete Illustrated Guide to Palmistry, 1998
A modern practical guide; the source of several framings used here for line quality and the relationship lines.
Johnny Fincham, The Spellbinding Power of Palmistry, 2005
A contemporary, psychologically framed approach that explicitly contests some inherited readings, including the counting of relationship lines. Representative of current practice.

Beyond the Western tradition

Indian palmistry, Hasta Samudrika Shastra, and Chinese hand reading share the same physical hand and many of the same landmarks but frame them within their own systems, and their readings do not always agree with the Western ones reported here. Where a chapter marks a point of genuine disagreement, it is noted in an Across traditions panel rather than smoothed over. Brief, accurate acknowledgement is better than forced comparison, and this book does not claim expertise it does not have in those systems.

A note on what is not cited

Associations given without a name are common to the Western tradition and appear, in some form, across all five references above. Prevalence claims of the kind "most hands show" have been avoided throughout, because no reliable survey supports them. Where this book says a feature is present on some hands and absent on many, that is the extent of the claim.

The free lessons

The twenty-five chapters of this book correspond to the twenty-five lessons of the learning path at palmistrypath.com/learn, where each carries its plates, its sources, and its checkpoint. The site is the living version of this course; the book is the one you can read by lamplight.

Reading for others

If you have worked through this book and its exercises, you are ready to read for a friend who has asked you to. Reread chapter 25 first. Ask before you begin, describe before you interpret, use the tradition's own words, and stop short of anything that sounds like a prediction. A reading given that way leaves the other person more curious about themselves, which is the best thing palmistry can do.
