Two head lines can be the same length, start at the same point, and still read very differently — because of the direction they travel. One runs level across the palm; another dips down toward the base. That difference in path is one of the head line's clearest features to observe, and one with its own traditional associations, separate from how long the line is or where it begins.

If you are working through the head line for the first time, the main [head line guide](/blog/beginner/head-line/) covers the full range of its traditional associations — starting point, path, depth, endpoint, and the writer's fork — and provides the context that makes any individual feature more useful. This article focuses specifically on path: whether the line runs straight or curves downward, and what that direction has traditionally been read to suggest.

## Where to look

The head line runs horizontally across the middle of the palm, below the heart line, beginning near the thumb side of the hand and travelling toward the outer edge. Path is about the course that journey takes: does the line hold a level, roughly horizontal direction as it crosses the palm, or does it angle downward, descending toward the base of the palm near the Mount of Luna?

The [overview of palm lines](/blog/beginner/what-do-palm-lines-mean/) and [How to Read a Palm](/blog/beginner/how-to-read-a-palm/) cover how to locate the major lines if you're still getting oriented.

## Telling the two apart

Path is read comparatively, against the palm itself, rather than against a fixed angle or measurement.

**Follow the line's general direction, not small waverings.** A head line's course is not a perfectly straight ruled line. Look at its overall course from start to terminus rather than any single short segment.

**A straight-running line stays roughly level.** It crosses the palm on a course close to horizontal, without a pronounced downward turn toward the base.

**A curved or sloping line angles down toward the outer, lower palm.** As it travels, it descends — sometimes gently, sometimes more steeply — toward the Mount of Luna at the base of the outer palm.

**There is no fixed angle or zone that marks the boundary between the two.** This article treats path as a comparative reading — straighter versus more sloped — not as a line that crosses a specific threshold.

## What it's traditionally associated with

William G. Benham, writing in *The Laws of Scientific Hand Reading* (1900), associated the direction of the head line with different mental tendencies.

A straight-running head line, Benham wrote, shows the operation of a practical mentality — a mind that works with what is concrete and observable. This article uses that narrow reading: a straight head line is traditionally associated with practical thinking, not with a claim that the mind behind it is stronger or weaker than any other.

A head line sloping toward the Mount of Luna, Benham wrote, indicates that the subject is not entirely material in their ideas and has the power of imagination — a mind that moves toward the inventive and the abstract rather than staying fixed on the immediately practical. Traditionally, this reading marks a departure from purely material or practical thought, not a rating of talent or intelligence.

Benham also noted, among people whose head lines dropped toward Luna, examples of writers, speakers, and others working in imaginative or literary fields. That is worth mentioning as the kind of example Benham drew on to illustrate the association — not as a rule that a steeper slope produces a stronger or more artistic mind. Benham's reading supports a slope-toward-imagination association; it does not establish that degree of slope scales with degree of creativity, and this article does not make that claim.

## Path in context

Path is one of several variables the head line lesson treats separately — alongside the line's starting point, its length, its depth and clarity, and its endpoint. Reading the direction of the line does not require folding in those other features, and this article does not combine them into a new reading specific to a straight or sloping line.

**Length** is its own variable, covered in the companion articles on the [short head line](/blog/beginner/short-head-line-meaning/) and [long head line](/blog/beginner/long-head-line-meaning/). A line can be straight and short, straight and long, sloping and short, or sloping and long — length and path are read side by side, not merged.

**Depth and clarity** are also separate, covered in the main head line guide, independent of whether the line runs level or slopes.

**The endpoint** — including the terminal fork sometimes called the [writer's fork](/blog/beginner/forked-head-line-meaning/) — is likewise its own feature, covered in the main head line guide. Palmistry Path's approved sources do not set out a specific combined reading for a particular path together with a particular endpoint or hand shape, so this article does not assert one.

## Common myths and oversimplifications

**"A sloping head line means someone isn't practical at all."** Benham's reading is a matter of emphasis and direction, not an absolute switch. A sloping line is associated with imagination and a departure from purely material thought — not with an absence of practicality.

**"The steeper the slope, the more creative or artistic the person is."** This overstates what the historical reading supports. Benham associated a sloping line with imagination and noted creative and literary examples among people with drooping lines, but Benham's reading does not establish a graduated scale where a steeper slope means a stronger creative gift.

**"A straight head line means someone is unimaginative."** The traditional reading is practical mentality, not an absence of imagination. Practical and imaginative are the two associations this article covers; neither is read as a deficiency.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does a straight head line mean in palmistry?**
Traditionally, a straight, level-running head line is associated with practical mentality — a mind oriented toward the concrete and observable, following Benham's reading in *The Laws of Scientific Hand Reading* (1900).

**What does a curved or sloping head line mean?**
A head line that slopes downward toward the Mount of Luna is traditionally associated with imagination — a mind that moves beyond purely practical or material thinking, per Benham's reading.

**Does a steeper slope mean a more creative person?**
No. Benham's reading supports an association between a sloping line and imagination, but it does not establish that a steeper slope produces a stronger or more artistic result.

**Is a straight head line better than a sloping one, or the other way around?**
Neither. Practical and imaginative describe different traditional readings of mental orientation, not a ranking of one over the other.
