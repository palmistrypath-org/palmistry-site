At the point where the palm meets the wrist, look for one or more short horizontal lines crossing the joint. William G. Benham, writing in *The Laws of Scientific Hand Reading* (1900), calls these markings the Rascettes, or Bracelets — a distinct minor feature with its own historical interpretation, separate from anything read on the palm itself.

This article covers where Benham places the Rascettes, what he associates with them, and a place where his own account is more cautious than the older tradition he describes: he reports that earlier palmists used the bracelets to estimate a person's lifespan, and states plainly that he could not confirm that method and did not use it himself. This article draws on Benham's account only; other classical or contemporary writers may treat the Rascettes differently, but that is outside the evidence checked for this piece. If you're new to the palm's secondary markings as a category, the [minor lines overview](/blog/beginner/minor-lines-overview) is useful context before reading on.

## Where to find it

The Rascettes sit at the crease or creases where the hand meets the forearm, just below the palm itself rather than on its reading surface. Benham describes hands showing sometimes three of these lines and sometimes only one; he presents this as something he observed, not as a rule about how many lines any given hand should have. The line closest to the palm — the first bracelet — is the one Benham treats as carrying interpretive weight. His account does not give a comparable reading for a second or third line beyond noting that they may be present.

## What it's traditionally associated with

Benham's central reading concerns the clarity of the first bracelet. He historically associates a deep, well-formed first bracelet with a strong constitution. Where the line is poorly marked, chained, or broad and shallow rather than clean and defined, his reading runs the other way, toward a weaker historical reading of the same constitutional quality.

This is a historical palmistry claim from a single named source, not a medical assessment. See "Palmistry Path's approach," below, for how this site separates that period reading from a health claim.

### Branches rising from the Rascettes

Benham also gives a historical reading to short branches that rise upward from the Rascettes toward the palm: he associates them with ambition, or an upward-striving quality in the person's character. This reading is specific to Benham's own account of this particular marking; it is not extended here to branches on any other line.

### The old lifespan-count method — and why Benham didn't use it

Older palmists, predating Benham's own writing, assigned a fixed span — Benham reports roughly thirty years — to each bracelet, using the count and position of the lines to estimate how long a person would live. Benham records this older method in his text, but he is explicit that he could not confirm it and that he did not use the Rascettes as a measure of lifespan in his own practice.

That distinction matters for how this article handles the topic: the older arithmetic is a real historical claim from the tradition Benham describes, and this article isn't pretending otherwise. But the source most directly on point here — Benham himself — declined to endorse or use it. Palmistry Path's own position, covered next, goes further than Benham's caution.

## Palmistry Path's approach

Benham's constitution and lifespan material is presented above exactly as what it is: a named historical writer's period reading, from 1900, of a hand feature. Palmistry Path does not treat wrist creases, or any other palm marking, as a way to diagnose health, assess vitality, or predict lifespan. Nothing in this article should be read as medical guidance. Where the historical record includes a predictive claim — like the older thirty-year-per-bracelet method above — this site names it plainly rather than pretending the tradition never made it, and then states, separately, why it doesn't repeat it: Palmistry Path's editorial position is that a wrist crease is not a diagnostic or predictive tool, and this site does not use palm markings to forecast lifespan or any other life outcome.

## Rascettes in context

This article keeps to what Benham states about the Rascettes on their own terms. In the material checked for this piece, he does not combine a Rascette reading with the life line, the mounts, travel lines, or hand type into a single joint interpretation, so this article does not invent one. If you're working with a hand where the Rascettes are present, the most defensible approach — consistent with the single source behind this article — is to note them as their own feature rather than folding them into a reading of a different line.

## Common myths

**"Each bracelet marks off about thirty years of your life."** This is the older method Benham himself reports without confirming or using. Even within the historical tradition, the source most specific to this feature declined to endorse it, and Palmistry Path does not use any palm marking to predict lifespan.

**"Wrist lines can tell you about your health or vitality."** Benham's constitution reading is a period historical claim, not a health assessment. Nothing on this site should be read as medical guidance, and no palm marking here is used to diagnose or assess a reader's health.

**"Everyone has, or should have, exactly three Rascettes."** Benham's own text notes that the count can vary — sometimes three, sometimes one — as an observation, not a rule about a normal or ideal number. This article doesn't treat any particular count as standard.

**"Branches rising from the bracelets are a bad sign."** In Benham's account, the opposite is closer to the mark: he associates these upward branches with ambition, not a negative quality.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the Rascettes in palmistry?**
Short horizontal lines crossing the wrist where it meets the palm. William G. Benham, writing in 1900, names them the Rascettes, or Bracelets, and treats the line closest to the palm — the first bracelet — as the one with the most interpretive weight in his system.

**Does the number of bracelets mean anything?**
Benham notes that a hand may show sometimes three and sometimes only one, but he presents this as an observation rather than a scored or ranked reading. His interpretation centers on the clarity of the first bracelet, not on a count.

**Can palmistry tell you how long you'll live from your wrist lines?**
No. Older palmists used a thirty-year-per-bracelet method to estimate lifespan, but Benham himself — the source this article relies on — says he could not confirm it and didn't use it. Palmistry Path does not read any palm marking, including the Rascettes, as a prediction of lifespan.

**What does a clear first bracelet mean in Benham's system?**
Benham historically associates a deep, well-formed first bracelet with a strong constitution, and a poorly marked, chained, or shallow one with the opposite reading. This is presented here as his period interpretation, not as a statement about a reader's actual health.

**What do the small branches near the Rascettes mean?**
Benham associates short branches rising from the Rascettes with ambition, or an upward-striving quality, specific to his account of this marking.
