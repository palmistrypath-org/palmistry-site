# Editorial Style Guide — Palmistry Path

*Governing reference for all written content on palmistrypath.com. Read before writing any article, lesson, glossary entry, or page copy.*

---

## 1. Brand Voice

Palmistry Path teaches palmistry as a structured, observational, and interpretive practice. The voice reflects that mission:

| Quality | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Educational** | Explain how and why, not just what. Give readers the tools to observe and interpret themselves. |
| **Grounded** | Every claim traces to a named tradition and, where possible, a named source. No invented meanings. |
| **Curious** | Treat the subject with genuine interest. Disagreement between traditions is interesting, not a problem to paper over. |
| **Respectful** | Honour the cultural origins of each tradition. Do not flatten, exoticise, or dismiss. |
| **Atmospheric** | The site's aesthetic is mystical — candlelit, evocative. The writing should feel like it belongs there, without becoming credulous or salesy. |

**Not:** fortune-telling, prediction, pop-psychology generalisation, mystical hype, or clinical detachment.

The test: would a thoughtful reader with no interest in palmistry finish an article feeling they had learned something real about a cultural tradition and its interpretive logic? If yes, the voice is right.

---

## 2. Phrasing: Permitted and Forbidden

Use hedged, attribution-aware language throughout. The framing signals intellectual honesty — it is not excessive caution, it is accuracy.

| Instead of… | Write… |
|---|---|
| "this line means…" | "traditionally associated with…" |
| "this indicates…" | "often interpreted as…" |
| "this shows that…" | "may suggest…" |
| "your heart line reveals…" | "the heart line, in this context, is read as…" |
| "palmistry proves…" | "palmistry holds that…" / "the tradition reads this as…" |
| "science shows palmistry works" | (do not write this — it is not a claim this site makes) |

**Forbidden phrases (never use):**

- "palmistry predicts…"
- "your future…" (as a statement of what will happen)
- "this guarantees…"
- "definitely means…"
- "will happen…"
- Any phrase that treats a palmistry reading as a medical, legal, or financial diagnosis
- "studies show" or "research confirms" in relation to palmistry claims (no credible peer-reviewed science supports palmistry as a predictive system)

---

## 3. Forbidden Claims

These are hard stops regardless of how popular the claim is elsewhere on the internet:

- **Predictive claims:** Never state that a line or feature predicts a specific future event (illness, death, divorce, wealth).
- **Medical claims:** Never suggest that palmistry identifies, diagnoses, or indicates medical conditions. Acknowledge historical writers (Benham, notably) who discussed potential physical correlates, but do so within the historical framing and with an explicit note that this article does not offer health interpretations.
- **Relationship predictions:** Never claim that a line or marking predicts the number of relationships, marriages, or children a person will have. This framing is widespread in popular palmistry and is not grounded in any major classical tradition.
- **Lifespan predictions:** Never suggest the life line indicates how long a person will live. This is one of the most persistent myths in popular palmistry and one of the most important to correct. State clearly that the tradition has never supported this reading.
- **Talent/destiny determinations:** Avoid telling a reader what they are or who they will become. Palmistry describes tendencies and orientations; it does not determine character or destiny.

## 4. Handling Traditions

Palmistry Path draws on three primary traditions: Western palmistry (especially Cheiro's system and the Benham school), Indian palmistry (Hasta Samudrika Shastra), and Chinese palmistry.

**Identify the tradition you are drawing from.** Never blend traditions into a unified claim without noting the blend. If Cheiro and the Indian tradition agree, name both and note the agreement. If they disagree, name both and present both — with a note on the difference, not a forced synthesis.

**Naming conventions:**

- Indian tradition: *Hasta Samudrika Shastra* (use Sanskrit terms accurately; introduce them with a translation or brief gloss on first use per article)
- Chinese tradition: note the Taoist cosmological framework where relevant (e.g., *Tian Di Ren* for the three major lines); name concepts accurately without over-explaining
- Western tradition: cite named authors rather than "Western palmistry says" where possible — "Cheiro noted," "Benham observed," "Gettings described"

**Gender conventions in tradition:**

- In **historical, foundational, or tradition-comparing articles:** acknowledge classical gendered practices (e.g., traditional Chinese left/right hand reading by sex) honestly and within their historical frame. Do not dismiss them; do not present them as current universal practice.
- In **practical, line-by-line, mount-by-mount, or how-to articles:** default to "active hand / passive hand" or "dominant / non-dominant hand." Do not relitigate historical context in every practical article.
- **Exception:** if a gendered distinction genuinely changes the interpretive content (not just the framing), surface it explicitly.

**Brevity principle:** Two accurate sentences about a non-primary tradition are more credible than an explanatory paragraph. Name it, state what it adds, move on. Trust the reader.

## 5. Sources

### 5.1 Source tiers

Every citation on this site must sit in one of four tiers. Tier decides what a source is allowed to carry, not merely whether it may be mentioned.

**Tier 1 — Primary and historical source texts.** The tradition speaking in its own voice. Cite by work and edition.

| Author | Work | Year | Tradition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cheiro (William John Warner) | *Palmistry for All* | 1916 | Western |
| Cheiro | *Cheiro's Language of the Hand* | 1894/1897 | Western |
| William G. Benham | *The Laws of Scientific Hand Reading* | 1900 | Western |
| Comte de Saint-Germain | *The Practice of Palmistry* | 1897 | Western |
| Stanislas d'Arpentigny | *La Chirognomonie* | 1843 | Western |
| Varāhamihira | *Bṛhat Saṃhitā*, chs. 68–70 (*puruṣa-lakṣaṇa*) | 6th c. CE | Indian |
| — | *Shenxiang quanbian* and the Chinese physiognomy compendia | Ming, from earlier material | Chinese |

**Tier 2 — Serious secondary and reference works.** Modern practitioner-scholars and academic treatments of the traditions.

| Author | Work | Year |
|---|---|---|
| Fred Gettings | *The Book of the Hand* | 1965 |
| Andrew Fitzherbert | *Hand Psychology* | 1986 |
| Peter West | *The Complete Illustrated Guide to Palmistry* | 1998 |
| Johnny Fincham | *The Spellbinding Power of Palmistry* | 2005 |
| Ellen Goldberg & Doris Dobkins | *The Art and Science of Hand Reading* | 2016 |
| Kenneth G. Zysk | *The Indian System of Human Marks* (Brill) | 2016 |
| Livia Kohn | "A Textbook of Physiognomy: The Tradition of the *Shenxiang quanbian*", *Asian Folklore Studies* 45:2 | 1986 |
| Richard J. Smith | *Fortune-tellers and Philosophers: Divination in Traditional Chinese Society* | 1991 |

**Tier 3 — Targeted reputable factual references.** Used where palmistry literature is not the right authority: anatomy, embryology, general history, etymology, cultural background. Peer-reviewed papers, university and museum publications, government and medical reference bodies, and academic encyclopaedias (e.g. the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*). Cite the specific work, not the genre.

**Tier 4 — Discovery only.** Commercial astrology and palmistry sites, SEO content farms, unsourced palmistry blogs, listicles, scraped or aggregated sites, AI-generated summaries, social posts. These may help you locate terminology, spot a claim worth chasing, or find a lead to a real source. **They are never cited, never named in body prose as an authority, and never appear in a source footer.** If a claim rests only on Tier 4 material, the claim does not run.

**Wikipedia and general encyclopaedias** sit outside the tiers as discovery aids. Use them to find the underlying source, then cite that source. Do not name an encyclopaedia in body prose as the authority for a meaningful or disputed claim.

### 5.2 Rules for sourcing

- Do not invent palmistry meanings, and do not invent source support. If a claim cannot be traced to a named tradition or source at Tier 1–3, cut it or qualify it explicitly as popular modern practice.
- Attribute specific claims to specific authors and works: "Benham described…", "Cheiro, writing in *Palmistry for All* (1916), noted…"
- **Quotation integrity.** Quotation marks mean verbatim wording that has been checked in the cited edition. If the wording has not been verified, paraphrase without quotation marks. Do not invent page or chapter precision. Where a much-repeated maxim cannot be located in the text it is usually attributed to, present it as a traditional formulation rather than as an author's words.
- **Tradition-specific sourcing is required.** Claims presented as Chinese tradition need a Chinese-tradition source; claims presented as Indian tradition need an Indian-tradition source. A Western author is not an authority on Chinese or Sanskrit material, and a Western bibliography does not support a tradition-internal claim. Where no tradition-appropriate source can be reached, say the claim reflects contemporary popular practice, or drop it.
- Distinguish historical and textual claims from modern popular practice. Do not present modern web folklore as ancient doctrine.
- Note genuine disagreement between sources and between traditions. Do not synthesise a false consensus, and do not reconcile traditions on the site's own authority.
- Phrase traditional associations as traditional: "traditionally associated with", "often interpreted as", "read in the tradition as". Never as fact about the reader.
- Transliteration and name variants (Sanskrit, Chinese, romanisation systems) should be given once with a note that forms vary, rather than presented as a single fixed spelling.
- Sources appear at the foot of every article in the standard italic format (see article template), most authoritative first, semicolon-separated, with author, italicised title, and year.

**No broad web research.** Targeted lookups to verify specific claims are expected and encouraged, especially for Tier 3 factual material and for tradition-specific sourcing. Do not scrape the internet for palmistry content to synthesise into an article.

## 6. Titles and Meta Descriptions

**Titles:**

- Be descriptive and specific: the reader should know what the article covers before clicking.
- Do not promise predictions or outcomes: "Heart Line Meaning: What It Says About Your Future" is forbidden.
- Use natural language that matches how someone actually searches: "Heart Line Meaning in Palmistry: A Beginner Guide" is a good model.
- Include the subject and context: "Mount of Jupiter Meaning in Palmistry" — not just "Jupiter."
- Avoid clickbait qualifiers: "shocking," "secrets," "hidden truth," "what they don't tell you."
- Maximum ~60 characters for the title tag (SEO); the displayed H1 can be slightly longer if needed.

**Meta descriptions:**

- One or two complete sentences, 140–160 characters.
- Describe what the reader will learn, using plain language.
- Do not make predictive promises.
- Match the article's actual content — do not oversell.
- Example (heart line): *"Learn what the heart line is, where to find it, and how palmistry traditions interpret its length, curve, depth, and endpoints."*

## 7. FAQs

FAQs serve two purposes: they answer the short-form queries that arrive from search, and they correct the most widespread myths associated with the topic.

**Structure:**

- Use a `## Frequently asked questions` heading (or `## Frequently Asked Questions` — consistent with the rest of the article's case convention).
- Each question is in bold: `**Where is the heart line?**`
- The answer follows immediately in plain paragraph text, without a sub-heading.
- 3–6 questions per article is typical; more than 8 risks diluting focus.

**Content rules for FAQs:**

- Answer the question directly first, then add nuance if needed.
- Correct common myths within the FAQ answers where relevant — this is the appropriate place to do so, alongside the myths section.
- Never use the FAQ to introduce claims that weren't covered in the main article body.
- Do not make predictive claims in FAQ answers. The same standards apply throughout.
- Questions should reflect queries real readers are likely to ask — not hypothetical questions invented to pad length.

## 8. Internal Linking

Internal links help readers navigate the learning path and signal topical authority to search engines. They also prevent readers from landing on one article and leaving without understanding that there is more to explore.

**Rules:**

- Link to relevant articles when they provide necessary context the current article does not provide.
- Use natural anchor text that describes what the linked article covers: `[the overview of the major lines](/blog/beginner/major-lines-overview)` — not "click here" or "this article."
- Do not link to the same page more than once per article.
- Priority linking targets: the `how-to-read-a-palm` article (context for any line/mount article aimed at beginners), the relevant `major-lines-overview` or `mounts-overview` (for individual feature articles), and related lesson pages via `relatedLesson` in the frontmatter.
- Do not stack multiple links in a single sentence.
- Internal links should add genuine value — link when it helps the reader, not to inflate link density.

**`relatedLesson` frontmatter field:** when a lesson page exists for the topic, set `relatedLesson` in the frontmatter to render the "Go deeper" CTA block at the bottom of the article. This is the primary cross-content bridge between blog articles and the Learn curriculum.

## 9. CTAs (Calls to Action)

Palmistry Path does not currently have a product to sell. CTAs should support learning and navigation, not generate false urgency or manufactured social proof.

**Permitted:**
- End-of-article navigation: directing readers to the next article in a sequence, or to a related article. ("The head line is the next major line in this series.")
- `relatedLesson` CTA block: rendered automatically when `relatedLesson` is set in frontmatter.
- Glossary links: invite readers to the glossary for term lookups.
- Email/guide CTAs (when the Guide page is live): plain and honest — "a free beginner's guide," not "unlock the secrets of your palm."

**Forbidden:**
- False urgency ("limited time," "before it's too late")
- Invented social proof ("thousands of readers have…")
- Predictive lures ("find out what your palm reveals about your future")
- Affiliate links that have not been explicitly approved

## 10. Medical, Legal, and Financial Disclaimers

**The standing disclaimer** (should appear in the site footer and About page):

> *Palmistry Path is for education, personal reflection, and exploration of a centuries-old interpretive tradition. It is not a substitute for professional medical, legal, or financial advice.*

**In articles:** you do not need to reprint the full disclaimer in every article. However:

- If an article's topic is close to health (e.g., cardiac associations of the heart line, the life line's length), include a brief in-article note: "Nothing in this article should be read as medical guidance." This is not boilerplate — include it only when the topic warrants it.
- Never suggest that palmistry readings should inform health, legal, or financial decisions.
- When discussing historical authors who made health-adjacent claims (Benham most notably), frame these as observations from the historical tradition, not current medical guidance, and state clearly that this article does not offer health interpretations.

## 11. Relationship and Lifespan Prediction Boundaries

These deserve their own section because they are so persistently misrepresented in popular palmistry.

**Relationships:**

- The marriage/relationship lines (on the percussion edge of the hand, below the little finger) do not indicate the number of marriages or significant relationships. They are traditionally associated with the quality or character of close relationships, not a count.
- Breaks, islands, and branches on the heart line do not count relationships.
- Never frame any line marking as predicting romantic heartbreak, divorce, or relationship failure.

**Lifespan:**

- The life line does not indicate how long a person will live. This is the most persistent myth in popular palmistry and directly contradicts the classical tradition.
- Benham, Cheiro, and Gettings all address this directly: the life line indicates the quality of vitality, energy, and life force — not its duration.
- Short life lines on hands of people who lived to old age, and long life lines on hands of people who died young, have been documented in the literature. The myth does not survive scrutiny.
- Always correct this myth explicitly in any article that covers the life line.

**Children:**

- Small lines off the relationship lines have historically been linked to children in some popular traditions. This association is not well-grounded in the major classical texts and should not be presented as established palmistry.
