## Email 2 — Which Hand to Read

**Subject:** Which hand do you read?  
**Preheader:** There's no single right answer — but the question matters  
**Send timing:** Day 3

---

One of the first questions beginners ask — and one that doesn't get a clean answer in most introductions — is: which hand do I look at?

The short version: both hands, but for different things.

Most traditions draw a distinction between what's often called the active and passive hand. In broad terms, the passive hand (typically the non-dominant hand) is traditionally associated with innate tendencies — the patterns you came in with. The active hand (your dominant hand) is often interpreted as reflecting how those tendencies have developed through experience and circumstance.

Some traditions weight the passive hand more heavily; others focus almost entirely on the active hand; a few treat both as equally important but read them separately, comparing the two as a kind of before-and-after. There is genuine disagreement here across Western, Indian, and Chinese palmistry, and I won't paper over it.

What I'd suggest for beginners: start with your dominant hand. It tends to show more developed line definition, which makes it easier to read while you're still learning what you're looking at. Once you've spent time with one hand, the comparison between the two becomes genuinely interesting.

There's a full article on this on the site, and a foundations lesson that goes into the tradition-by-tradition differences in more detail.

## Email 3 — What the Lines Mean

**Subject:** What do the lines actually mean?  
**Preheader:** They're not a script — they're a map  
**Send timing:** Day 6

---

Before we get into the lines themselves, it's worth saying something about what a palm line is and isn't.

Lines are not a fixed script written before you were born. That's a popular image, and it has a long history in fortune-telling traditions, but it's not the most useful frame for study. What lines actually are — as physical features — is the result of how your hand folds and flexes over time, shaped by genetics, development, and habit. What palmistry traditions have done, across centuries and cultures, is observe patterns in those features and draw inferences from them.

With that frame in place: there are three lines that appear on almost every hand and that every major tradition addresses. They're typically called the heart line, the head line, and the life line — though terminology varies. Each is traditionally associated with a broad area of human experience: emotional life, mental patterns, and vitality or life path, respectively.

Whether those inferences reflect something real about a person, or something the observer brings to the reading, is a genuinely open question. The study is interesting either way.

The life line, in particular, is frequently misread by beginners as indicating lifespan. That's a persistent misconception. It's more accurately described as traditionally associated with constitution, energy, and the quality of life experience — not its length.

## Email 4 — Hand Shapes and Mounts

**Subject:** There's more to a hand than lines  
**Preheader:** Hand shapes, mounts, and a broader view  
**Send timing:** Day 10

---

This is a good moment to pull back from the lines and look at the hand as a whole.

Take a look at your hand now — not at the lines yet, but at the overall shape. Is the palm roughly square or rectangular? Are the fingers long relative to the palm, or shorter? Is the skin texture fine or coarse? These observations belong to a different part of palmistry than line reading, and in some traditions they're considered foundational — something you read before you look at anything else.

Western palmistry has, for roughly a century, used a four-type system for hand shapes: Earth, Air, Fire, and Water. Each shape is traditionally associated with a broad temperament and a characteristic way of engaging with the world. The system is approximate — most hands don't fit neatly into one category — but it's a useful lens for getting a first impression of a hand before you start reading details.

Alongside hand shape, there are the mounts: raised pads of flesh at the base of each finger and along the edges of the palm. Each mount is traditionally associated with a planet or archetype and with the qualities that planet has carried in Western occult tradition. A developed mount may suggest prominence of those qualities; a flat or absent mount is often read as less emphasis in that area.

None of this is meant to replace line reading — it complements it. A full hand reading, in most traditions, draws on shape, mounts, lines, fingers, and thumbs together.

## Email 5 — Worksheet Pack Teaser

**Subject:** The gap between knowing and seeing  
**Preheader:** Why looking at a hand is harder than reading about one  
**Send timing:** Day 14

---

There's a particular kind of frustration that comes a few weeks into learning palmistry.

You've read about the heart line. You know, roughly, where it sits and what it's traditionally associated with. You understand the difference between the active and passive hand. You've looked at a diagram or two.

And then you sit down with an actual hand — yours, or someone else's — and the lines don't look like the diagrams. The mounts don't announce themselves. You can't tell where one line ends and another begins, or whether that faint mark counts as a line at all.

This is normal. It's not a gap in your knowledge — it's a gap in your observation skills, and those are a different thing entirely. Reading about a hand and looking at a hand are almost separate disciplines. The second one takes practice, and practice needs structure.
